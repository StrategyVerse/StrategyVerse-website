Sustainability Communications: How to Talk About ESG Without Greenwashing

By Praveen Singh  |  Founder & Chief Strategist, Strategyverse Consulting  |  9 min read
Back to Insights
Praveen Singh 9 min read

There is a particular kind of corporate communication that now does more harm than good, and it is the sustainability claim that nobody quite believes: a brand splashes green leaves across its packaging, declares itself "committed to a better planet", and pledges to reach carbon neutrality by some comfortably distant year that no current executive will still be around to answer for. A decade ago a gesture like that might have earned a little goodwill, but today it mostly earns eye-rolls, and increasingly it earns something more dangerous than that, which is the close attention of journalists and regulators who have learned exactly what to look for.

The problem was never that companies talk about sustainability, because a great many of them are doing genuinely valuable work and have every right in the world to tell that story; the problem is the widening gap between what brands claim and what they can actually prove, and audiences have become remarkably good at sensing precisely where that gap opens up. Communicating your ESG efforts well has quietly become a discipline of restraint and evidence at least as much as one of ambition, and getting it wrong now tends to be worse than having said nothing at all.

So here is how to talk about sustainability in a way that people are actually willing to believe.

Understand Why Greenwashing Backfires So Badly

Greenwashing is not only an ethical problem, it is a strategic one, and the reason is that the punishment for it is both severe and oddly specific: when a brand overstates its environmental credentials and is caught doing it, the damage never stays neatly contained to the one exaggerated claim, but spreads outward and calls every other statement the company has ever made into question, because once an audience learns that you stretched the truth about your packaging, the obvious next thought is to wonder what else you have been stretching.

This is really just the dynamic that governs all reputation, where trust is built slowly and lost with astonishing speed, and a single exposed exaggeration can undo years of perfectly genuine effort in an afternoon. Regulators across a growing number of markets are now actively pursuing misleading environmental claims, journalists have made a minor genre out of the corporate greenwashing takedown, and social media guarantees that a caught-out claim travels further and faster than the original ever did. The asymmetry is brutal, because a modest and provable claim earns you modest, durable credibility, whereas an inflated one risks a very public unravelling that can turn into a reputational crisis in its own right, exactly the kind we discuss in our guide to managing your online reputation before a search result quietly defines you.

Lead With Evidence, Not Adjectives

The most important shift you can make toward credible sustainability communication is simply to replace adjectives with evidence, because words like "eco-friendly", "green", "clean", "responsible" and "sustainable" now signal almost nothing at all, precisely because everybody reaches for them and hardly anybody backs them up, so that the moment an audience reads one without any proof attached to it, a small alarm goes off somewhere in the back of their mind.

Specific, verifiable facts do exactly the opposite of that, because a sentence like "we cut water use in our manufacturing by 34% between 2023 and 2025, verified by an independent audit" is something a sceptic can actually go and check, and it is that very checkability that makes it persuasive in the first place. Numbers, timeframes, baselines and third-party verification carry a weight that no adjective ever will, so whenever you find yourself tempted to describe your own work as "green", it is worth stopping to ask what the underlying number or fact actually is and leading with that instead. If it turns out there is no number to be found, that absence is itself a useful signal that the claim may simply not be ready to make yet.

Be Honest About the Journey

It sounds counterintuitive, but the most credible sustainability communication is usually the kind that openly admits what has not been solved yet, because very few companies genuinely have a clean record and most are somewhere partway along a journey, making real progress in some areas while wrestling with entirely real trade-offs in others, and audiences understand this perfectly well. What they are actually looking for is not some impossible perfection but plain honesty about where a company really stands.

A brand that says, in effect, "here is what we have achieved, here is what we are still working on, and here is the part that remains genuinely hard for us" comes across as far more believable than one projecting a flawless, frictionless virtue that nobody in the real world possesses. Acknowledging a trade-off openly, admitting that your product carries an environmental cost you are actively working to bring down, for instance, signals that you take the issue seriously rather than treating it as a coat of marketing paint. That kind of honesty is also, in its own way, good storytelling, because the most compelling brand stories are always the ones that feel true precisely because they leave the messy, unresolved parts in, rather than the airbrushed version that ends up reading as too clean to trust.

Focus on What Is Material, Not What Is Flattering

One of the oldest greenwashing patterns is to trumpet some small, feel-good initiative while staying carefully silent about a far larger negative impact, and you know the examples as well as I do, whether it is the airline making much of its recycled cabin cups or the oil company advertising its comparatively tiny renewables division. Audiences and journalists tend to see straight through this sort of thing, and because it reads as a deliberate attempt at distraction, it usually ends up doing more damage than simply saying nothing would have.

Credible communication does the harder thing instead, which is to focus on your most material impacts, the places where your business genuinely affects the environment or society most, and to address them head on. It takes more courage, since your biggest impacts are so often your hardest problems, but it is the only approach that actually survives scrutiny, so if your largest footprint sits in your supply chain then the supply chain is what you should be talking about, even when the honest story there is one of gradual progress rather than outright triumph. Choosing to communicate about what matters most rather than what flatters most is, in itself, a powerful signal of credibility.

Let Others Carry the Message

The least persuasive possible voice on the subject of your own sustainability is your own advertising, while the most persuasive voices are always the independent ones, the certification bodies and credible third-party ratings, the journalists who have examined your claims and come away satisfied, the customers who can speak to a real outcome they have actually experienced. This is the same fundamental reason that earned media builds trust in a way paid media simply cannot, and it applies with particular force to sustainability, where audiences arrive already primed to distrust anything that sounds like self-promotion.

Wherever you can manage it, then, try to structure your communication so that the proof arrives from somewhere outside your own marketing department, which in practice means pursuing recognised certifications and letting them do the talking, opening your data to independent verification and citing whoever did the verifying, giving journalists genuine access and enough real substance to write something credible, and putting forward the customers and partners who can describe the effect of your work in the real world. When the endorsement comes from a source that has no earthly incentive to flatter you, the claim stops sounding like a slogan and starts sounding like a fact.

Match Your Words to Your Timeline

Distant, vague targets have become a hallmark of greenwashing for the simple reason that they cost nothing to announce and are wonderfully easy to abandon quietly once the press cycle has moved on, which is why "net zero by 2050" coming from a company with no interim milestones and no visible action today reads as an aspiration dressed up in the costume of a commitment, and audiences increasingly treat it as exactly that.

Credible communication ties its ambition to a timeline you are demonstrably acting on right now, so that if you do hold a genuine long-term goal, you pair it with the specific near-term steps you are taking this year and the interim targets you are willing to be measured against along the way. There is all the difference in the world between a company that says "we aim to halve emissions by 2030, we cut them 12% last year, and here is precisely what we are doing this year" and one offering nothing but the far-off headline number, because the first is making a commitment while the second is merely making a wish, and that difference is glaringly obvious to anyone paying attention, which is precisely the group of people who scrutinise sustainability claims for a living.

The Underlying Principle

Every rule here really collapses into a single idea, which is to close the gap between what you say and what you actually do, because greenwashing at its heart was never about being imperfect at all, it was about the distance between the claim and the reality. Narrow that distance, by making a habit of claiming a little less than you can prove rather than a little more, and your sustainability communication becomes not merely safe but genuinely powerful.

The brands that will earn real credit for their environmental and social work over the coming years are not going to be the ones with the boldest slogans, but the ones whose modest, evidenced, honest claims hold up the moment somebody bothers to check, because in a landscape as crowded with green noise as this one, being provably true has quietly become the rarest and most valuable position a brand can hold.
