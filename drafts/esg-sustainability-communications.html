Sustainability Communications: How to Talk About ESG Without Greenwashing

By Praveen Singh  |  Founder & Chief Strategist, Strategyverse Consulting  |  9 min read
Back to Insights
Praveen Singh 9 min read

There is a specific kind of corporate communication that now does more harm than good: the sustainability claim nobody believes. A brand splashes green leaves across its packaging, announces it is "committed to a better planet", and pledges to be carbon neutral by some comfortably distant year. A decade ago this might have earned goodwill. Today it earns eye-rolls, and increasingly, it earns scrutiny from journalists and regulators who have learned exactly what to look for.

The problem is not that companies are talking about sustainability. Many are doing genuinely valuable work and have every right to tell that story. The problem is the widening gap between what brands claim and what they can prove, and audiences have become remarkably good at sensing that gap. Communicating your ESG efforts well is now a discipline of restraint and evidence as much as ambition, and getting it wrong is worse than staying quiet.

Here is how to talk about sustainability in a way people actually believe.

Understand Why Greenwashing Backfires So Badly

Greenwashing is not just an ethical problem; it is a strategic one, because the punishment is severe and specific. When a brand overstates its environmental credentials and gets caught, the damage is not limited to the exaggerated claim. It calls every other statement the company makes into question. If they stretched the truth about their packaging, what else are they stretching?

This is the same dynamic that governs all reputation: trust is built slowly and lost quickly, and a single exposed exaggeration can undo years of genuine effort. Regulators in many markets are now actively pursuing misleading environmental claims, journalists write dedicated takedowns of corporate greenwashing, and social media ensures a caught-out claim travels fast. The asymmetry is brutal. A modest, provable claim earns modest, durable credibility. An inflated claim risks a public unravelling that can become its own reputational crisis, the kind we discuss in our guide to managing your online reputation before a search result defines you.

Lead With Evidence, Not Adjectives

The single most important shift in credible sustainability communication is to replace adjectives with evidence. "Eco-friendly", "green", "clean", "responsible", and "sustainable" are words that now signal almost nothing, precisely because everyone uses them and few back them up. The moment an audience reads them without proof attached, a small alarm goes off.

Specific, verifiable facts do the opposite. "We reduced water use in our manufacturing by 34% between 2023 and 2025, verified by an independent audit" is a sentence a sceptic can check, and its checkability is exactly what makes it persuasive. Numbers, timeframes, baselines, and third-party verification carry weight that adjectives cannot. Whenever you are tempted to describe your work as "green", stop and ask what the underlying number or fact is, and lead with that instead. If there is no number, that is a useful signal that the claim may not be ready to make.

Be Honest About the Journey

Counterintuitively, the most credible sustainability communication admits what is not yet solved. Very few companies have a clean record. Most are somewhere on a journey, making real progress in some areas while grappling with genuine trade-offs in others. Audiences know this. What they are looking for is not perfection but honesty about where a company actually stands.

A brand that says "here is what we have achieved, here is what we are still working on, and here is what remains genuinely hard for us" is far more believable than one projecting flawless virtue. Acknowledging a trade-off, that your product has an environmental cost you are actively working to reduce, for instance, signals that you understand the issue seriously rather than treating it as a marketing veneer. This honesty is also a form of good storytelling: the most compelling brand stories are the ones that feel true because they include the messy, unresolved parts, not the airbrushed version that reads as too clean to trust.

Focus on What Is Material, Not What Is Flattering

A classic greenwashing pattern is to trumpet a small, feel-good initiative while staying silent about a much larger negative impact. The airline promoting its recycled cabin cups. The oil company advertising its modest renewables division. Audiences and journalists see straight through this, and it reads as a deliberate distraction, which is worse than saying nothing.

Credible communication focuses on your most material impacts, the areas where your business genuinely affects the environment or society most, and addresses them directly. This takes more courage, because your biggest impacts are often your hardest problems, but it is the only approach that survives scrutiny. If your largest footprint is in your supply chain, talk about your supply chain, even if the story there is one of progress rather than triumph. Choosing to communicate about what matters most, rather than what flatters most, is itself a credibility signal.

Let Others Carry the Message

The least persuasive voice on your sustainability is your own advertising. The most persuasive voices are independent ones: certification bodies, credible third-party ratings, journalists who have examined your claims and found them sound, customers who can speak to real outcomes. This is the fundamental reason earned media builds trust that paid media cannot, and it applies with particular force to sustainability, where audiences are primed to distrust self-promotion.

Where you can, structure your communication so that the proof comes from outside your marketing department. Pursue recognised certifications and let them speak. Open your data to independent verification and cite the verifier. Give journalists genuine access and enough substance to write a credible piece. Put forward customers and partners who can describe the real-world effect of your work. When the endorsement comes from a source with no incentive to flatter you, the claim stops sounding like a slogan and starts sounding like a fact.

Match Your Words to Your Timeline

Distant, vague targets have become a hallmark of greenwashing precisely because they cost nothing to announce and are easy to quietly abandon. "Net zero by 2050" from a company with no interim milestones and no current action is an aspiration dressed as a commitment, and audiences increasingly treat it that way.

Credible communication ties ambition to a timeline you are actually acting on now. If you have a long-term goal, pair it with the specific, near-term steps you are taking this year and the interim targets you will be measured against along the way. A company that says "we aim to halve emissions by 2030, we cut them 12% last year, and here is exactly what we are doing this year" is making a commitment. A company offering only the far-off headline number is making a wish. The difference is obvious to anyone paying attention, and the people who scrutinise sustainability claims are paying very close attention.

The Underlying Principle

Every rule here reduces to one idea: close the gap between what you say and what you do. Greenwashing is not fundamentally about being imperfect; it is about the distance between claim and reality. Narrow that distance, by claiming less than you can prove rather than more, and your sustainability communication becomes not just safe but genuinely powerful.

The brands that will earn credit for their environmental and social work over the coming years are not the ones with the boldest slogans. They are the ones whose modest, evidenced, honest claims hold up when someone checks, because in a landscape crowded with green noise, being provably true is the rarest and most valuable position a brand can hold.
