Media Training 101: How to Prepare Your Spokespeople for the Interview

By Praveen Singh  |  Founder & Chief Strategist, Strategyverse Consulting  |  8 min read
Back to Insights
Praveen Singh 8 min read

You spend weeks earning the interview, and then it all comes down to twenty minutes: the pitch has landed, the journalist is interested, a slot is finally in the diary, and your founder sits down in front of the camera and quietly undoes the whole thing. They ramble, they take the bait on a loaded question, they say something they did not quite mean, or they bury the one point that actually mattered underneath five that did not, and by the end the story that might have built your reputation is instead built around the single unfortunate sentence they wish they could take back.

This happens all the time, and the frustrating part is that it is almost always avoidable, because the difference between a spokesperson who strengthens the brand and one who damages it comes down far less often to intelligence or seniority than it does to plain preparation. A brilliant leader who has never been coached will lose, every time, to an average one who has, simply because the interview is a specific skill with its own rules, and none of those rules are remotely obvious to someone who spends their days running a business rather than fielding questions from reporters.

So here is how you prepare a spokesperson properly.

Start With Messages, Not Answers

Before you do anything else, work out what the spokesperson has to communicate no matter what they are actually asked, and keep the list short, because if it runs to ten points it is useless and what you really want is two or three: the handful of things that, even if nothing else survives the edit, would make the whole interview worthwhile. Write them down in plain, quotable language, the way a real person talks rather than the way a press release reads, so they come out naturally under pressure.

This matters so much because interviews are unpredictable and those messages are the only fixed point the spokesperson has to hold onto. A journalist can take the conversation absolutely anywhere, and a spokesperson without clear messages will simply follow it wherever it goes, answering each question in isolation and walking out having said an enormous amount while communicating nothing at all, whereas a spokesperson armed with three sharp messages always has somewhere to return to, again and again, however far the questions happen to wander. Everything else in media training exists, in the end, to serve those few messages.

Know Who You Are Talking To

A spokesperson should never walk into an interview cold, and the single most useful thing you can do beforehand is understand the specific journalist and the outlet they write for, which means knowing what the publication is and who actually reads it, what this particular reporter has covered recently and the angle they tend to take, whether they are likely to be friendly or neutral or openly adversarial, and whether you are handing over a quick quote for a news piece or sitting down for a long feature in which every stray remark is fair game.

All of that changes how the spokesperson ought to approach the entire conversation, because a trade-publication interview with a friendly reporter who has covered your sector for years is a completely different proposition from a national business journalist chasing a critical story, and knowing which one you are walking into strips out most of the surprises, which are exactly what tend to trip spokespeople up. This kind of preparation is really just part of a broader communications discipline, and it flows naturally out of having a clear PR strategy that has already defined your audiences and your messages long before any single interview lands in the calendar.

Rehearse the Questions You Are Dreading

Every organisation carries a small set of questions it privately hopes will never come up, whether that is the pricing that sits well above the competition, the customer who left and said so publicly, the regulatory grey area nobody wants to explain, or the founder's own past remark that has aged badly, and the natural instinct is to avoid thinking about any of them, which is precisely the wrong move. The questions you dread are the ones you have to rehearse hardest, because a journalist can smell an unprepared answer from across the table, and the moment a spokesperson gets flustered is the moment the whole interview slips quietly out of their hands.

So sit down and list every difficult question honestly, then work out a composed, truthful answer to each one, an answer that acknowledges the question fairly without feeding it and that finds its way back to solid ground. The aim is not to have a slick piece of deflection ready for everything, which audiences see through anyway, but simply to have thought the hard moments through in advance, so that when one of them actually arrives the spokesperson meets it calmly instead of improvising something regrettable under pressure. Preparation is what turns a dreaded question from a genuine threat into a moment you can handle.

Teach the Bridge

Bridging is the core skill of the whole discipline, and it is the clearest thing separating a trained spokesperson from an untrained one, because to bridge is to answer the question you were actually asked, honestly, and then move deliberately on to the point you came to make, using nothing more complicated than a simple connective phrase along the lines of "what really matters here is", "the bigger picture is", or "what we are seeing across the industry is".

The word doing all the work in that sentence is honestly, because bridging is emphatically not dodging: a spokesperson who reaches for a bridge in order to avoid answering just looks evasive, and a decent journalist will simply press harder until they do. Handle the question genuinely and then expand toward your message, though, and you manage to stay both responsive and in control at the same time, which is a surprisingly difficult balance to strike. It is a learned rhythm that feels awkward the first few times anyone tries it, and that is exactly why it has to be practised out loud rather than merely explained, until the spokesperson can do it without sounding like they are visibly running a technique.

Run a Real Mock Interview

Everything up to this point stays theoretical until the spokesperson has actually sat through a realistic rehearsal, which is why a proper mock interview, with someone playing a genuinely sceptical journalist and, ideally, a camera quietly running, is the single most valuable part of any media training session. It brings to the surface all the habits that no amount of briefing will ever reveal on paper: the nervous over-explaining, the small tell that gives away when a hard question has landed, the compulsion to keep talking well after the point has been made, the filler words, the defensive shift in body language.

Then you sit and watch the recording back together, which is deeply uncomfortable and enormously effective in roughly equal measure, because almost nobody believes they ramble or fidget or talk over their own best line until they are made to watch themselves do it. Reviewing the footage is what turns vague advice into specific, observable fixes, and a second mock interview run straight afterwards almost always shows a dramatic improvement on the first. If you take only one thing from this whole article and act on it, make it this one.

Coach the Delivery, Not Just the Words

What a spokesperson says obviously matters, but how they say it very often matters more, particularly on camera, because audiences read confidence and credibility off pace and pauses and tone and body language long before they have consciously processed a single word of the actual content, and a rushed, anxious delivery can quietly undermine even a genuinely perfect answer.

So coach the spokesperson to slow right down, to pause and think before answering rather than papering over the silence with an "um", and above all to stop talking the moment they have made their point instead of trailing off into the qualifications that dilute it or, worse still, drifting into territory they had no need to enter. A surprising number of the worst interview moments happen not because of anything the journalist asked but because the spokesperson simply kept going after they should have stopped, since silence is the reporter's oldest tool, and a trained spokesperson learns to sit comfortably in it rather than rushing to fill it.

Preparation Is Not Optional for High-Stakes Moments

Routine interviews benefit from all of this, but high-stakes moments genuinely depend on it, because the instant the situation becomes a crisis, an investigation, or any conversation in which a single wrong word carries real consequences, media training stops being a nice-to-have and turns into something close to essential. The same discipline that helps a founder glide through a clean product-launch interview is precisely what keeps them composed and credible when the questions turn hostile, which is exactly the point at which it matters most, so if your organisation could ever plausibly face that kind of scrutiny, the time to build the skill is now, as part of the broader readiness we set out in our guide to the crisis communication plan every business needs, well before you ever have to think about managing the fallout on social media.

The interview is the moment when all your patient PR work either pays off or comes apart in real time, and while earning the opportunity is genuinely hard, wasting it turns out to be remarkably easy. A few hours of honest preparation is about the cheapest insurance you will ever buy against your own spokesperson becoming exactly the wrong kind of headline.
