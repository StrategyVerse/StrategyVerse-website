Media Training 101: How to Prepare Your Spokespeople for the Interview

By Praveen Singh  |  Founder & Chief Strategist, Strategyverse Consulting  |  8 min read
Back to Insights
Praveen Singh 8 min read

You spend weeks earning the interview. The pitch lands, the journalist is interested, a slot gets scheduled, and then your founder sits down in front of the camera and undoes all of it in twenty minutes. They ramble. They take the bait on a loaded question. They say something they did not mean, or bury the one point that mattered under five that did not. The story that could have built your reputation ends up quoting the one unfortunate sentence instead.

This happens constantly, and it is almost always avoidable. The difference between a spokesperson who strengthens the brand and one who damages it is rarely intelligence or seniority. It is preparation. A brilliant leader who has never been coached will lose to an average one who has, because the interview is a specific skill with its own rules, and those rules are not obvious to someone who spends their day running a business.

Here is how to prepare a spokesperson properly.

Start With Messages, Not Answers

Before anything else, decide what the spokesperson must communicate regardless of what they are asked. Not ten things. Two or three. These are the points that, if they land nothing else, would make the interview a success. Write them down in plain, quotable language, the way a person actually speaks, not the way a press release reads.

This matters because interviews are unpredictable and messages are the anchor. A journalist can take the conversation anywhere, and a spokesperson without clear messages will follow wherever it goes, answering each question in isolation and leaving the interview having said a great deal and communicated nothing. A spokesperson with three sharp messages has somewhere to return to, again and again, no matter where the questions wander. Everything else in media training exists to serve those messages.

Know Who You Are Talking To

A spokesperson should never walk into an interview cold. The single most useful preparation is understanding the specific journalist and outlet. What publication is this? Who reads it? What has this particular journalist written recently, and what angle do they tend to take? Are they likely to be friendly, neutral, or adversarial? Is this a quick quote for a news piece or a long feature where every offhand remark is fair game?

The answers change how the spokesperson should approach the entire conversation. A trade publication interview with a friendly reporter who covers your sector is a very different situation from a national business journalist chasing a critical angle. Knowing which one you are walking into removes most of the surprises, and surprises are what trip spokespeople up. This kind of preparation is also part of a broader communications discipline; it flows naturally from having a clear PR strategy that defines your audiences and messages before any single interview.

Rehearse the Questions You Are Dreading

Every organisation has a set of questions it hopes will not come up. The pricing that is higher than competitors. The customer who left publicly. The regulatory grey area. The founder's controversial past statement. The instinct is to avoid thinking about these, which is exactly backwards. The questions you dread are the ones you must rehearse most, because a journalist can smell an unprepared answer, and the moment a spokesperson gets flustered is the moment the interview slips out of their control.

Sit down and list every difficult question honestly. Then work out a composed, truthful answer to each one, an answer that acknowledges the question without feeding it, and that finds a way back to solid ground. The goal is not to have a slick deflection for everything. It is to have thought through the hard moments in advance so that, when one arrives, the spokesperson meets it calmly instead of inventing something under pressure. Preparation converts a dreaded question from a threat into a manageable moment.

Teach the Bridge

Bridging is the core interview skill, and it is what separates trained spokespeople from untrained ones. To bridge is to answer the question you were asked, honestly, and then move deliberately to the point you want to make. It uses simple connective phrases: "What really matters here is...", "The bigger picture is...", "What we are seeing across the industry is...".

The key word is honestly. Bridging is not dodging. If a spokesperson uses a bridge to avoid answering, they look evasive and the journalist will press harder. But if they genuinely address the question and then expand toward their message, they stay responsive and in control at the same time. This is a learned rhythm, and it feels awkward the first few times. That is why it has to be practised out loud, not just explained, until the spokesperson can do it without sounding like they are running a technique.

Run a Real Mock Interview

Everything above is theory until the spokesperson has actually sat through a realistic rehearsal. A proper mock interview, with someone playing a genuinely sceptical journalist and ideally with a camera running, is the most valuable part of any media training session. It surfaces the habits that no amount of briefing will reveal: the nervous over-explaining, the tell when a hard question lands, the tendency to keep talking after the point is made, the filler words, the defensive body language.

Then watch the recording together. This is uncomfortable and enormously effective. People do not believe they ramble or fidget or undercut their own authority until they see it. Reviewing the footage turns vague advice into specific, observable fixes, and a second mock interview almost always shows dramatic improvement over the first. If you do only one thing from this article, do this.

Coach the Delivery, Not Just the Words

What a spokesperson says matters, but how they say it often matters more, especially on camera. Audiences read confidence and credibility from pace, pauses, tone, and body language long before they parse the actual content. A nervous, rushed delivery undermines even a perfect answer.

Coach the spokesperson to slow down. To pause before answering rather than filling silence with "um". To stop talking once they have made their point, instead of trailing into qualifications that dilute it or, worse, wander into dangerous territory. Many of the worst interview moments happen not because of a question but because the spokesperson kept talking after they should have stopped. Silence is the journalist's tool; a trained spokesperson learns not to rush in and fill it.

Preparation Is Not Optional for High-Stakes Moments

Routine interviews benefit from this preparation. High-stakes moments demand it. When the situation is a crisis, an investigation, or any interview where a wrong word carries real consequences, media training stops being a nice-to-have and becomes essential. The same discipline that helps a founder deliver a clean product-launch interview is what keeps them composed and credible when the questions turn hostile, which is exactly when it matters most. If your organisation could ever face that kind of scrutiny, the time to build the skill is now, as part of the broader readiness we describe in our guide to the crisis communication plan every business needs, well before you also need to think about managing the fallout on social media.

The interview is the moment all your PR work either pays off or falls apart. Earning the opportunity is hard. Wasting it is easy. A few hours of genuine preparation is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy against a spokesperson becoming the wrong kind of headline.
